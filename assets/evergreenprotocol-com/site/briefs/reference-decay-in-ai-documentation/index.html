Intelligence brief · Reference Decay Series · Brief 001

Reference Decay in AI Documentation

AI documentation — model cards, usage policies, knowledge bases, evaluation records — is operationally reference-grade: decisions, audits, and automated systems depend on it as valid at the moment they act on it. This brief examines four operational cases in which that documentation decays without visible failure.

What this brief examines

Enterprise AI documentation serves as the reference layer for governance decisions, audit trails, compliance records, and automated system behavior. Unlike general documentation, it is cited in governance processes, consumed by AI agents, embedded in decision records, and used to establish evidentiary chains that must withstand retrospective scrutiny.

When this documentation decays — when it silently becomes invalid, loses its version trace, or diverges from current operational reality — the governance structures that depend on it lose their foundation without visible failure. The document loads. The URL resolves. The reference appears complete.

This brief examines four cases:

  • The unversioned model card: what happens when a model card is updated in place without a version record.
  • The policy URL that still loads: a usage policy revised without versioning, cited in documents that assume currency.
  • The agent citation from stale knowledge: an AI agent retrieves a knowledge-base entry that no longer reflects the current governing constraint.
  • Definition divergence across departments: a governance term redefined in one document without updating others that depend on it.

These cases are not failure modes or security incidents. They are ordinary outcomes of AI documentation practices that lack version discipline and reference continuity governance.

Case 1 — The unversioned model card

Scenario. An enterprise deploys an AI assistant for regulatory advisory. The model card is published at a fixed internal URL. Two governance teams cite it in their decision records over the following month. Six weeks after deployment, the compliance team updates the model card to reflect changed input constraints and a revised risk category. The update is made in place. No prior version is preserved. The version field on the card is not incremented. No notification is sent to teams that have already cited it.

What the evidence trail shows. Both decision records cite the model card URL. The URL resolves. A third team, conducting an audit review of those decisions, retrieves the URL and receives the current, updated card. Their audit record reflects the current version as if it governed both prior decisions. It did not.

Governance consequence. The decisions made under the prior version of the model card cannot be reconstructed against the governing specification in force when they were made. The audit cannot establish what the model was evaluated against, what constraints were in place, or whether the risk category applied at that time. All of this is invisible from the URL, which continues to resolve without error.

Model cards without version control are not unusual. They are the default in most AI documentation practices. The governance gap they create is not a model risk — the model has not changed. It is a reference continuity risk: the record of what the model was at the point of governance decisions no longer exists.

Case 2 — The policy URL that still loads

Scenario. An enterprise publishes its AI usage policy at a fixed URL. An employee handbook, onboarding module, and two department governance documents all cite this URL. Fourteen months after initial publication, the policy is significantly revised: three new prohibited use categories are added and the approval authority for AI-generated advisory outputs is changed. The URL is not changed. The page is not versioned. A “Last reviewed” timestamp is updated. No changelog is published. No prior version is preserved.

What the evidence trail shows. The URL resolves. The page appears current. The timestamp confirms it has been maintained. Documents citing the URL appear to reference a current, maintained policy.

Governance consequence. An employee who completed onboarding prior to the revision cannot demonstrate that their prior usage complied with the policy in force when they onboarded — because that policy is gone. A compliance review cannot reconstruct what prohibited use categories applied at a prior date. A governance document citing the policy cannot establish which version of the policy it incorporated.

The policy URL did not break. It continued to resolve and appear maintained. But the reference it provided — to a specific set of rules that governed behavior at a specific time — decayed completely at the moment of the unversioned update. Every citation to that URL now points to a different document than the one cited.

Case 3 — The agent citation from stale knowledge

Scenario. A customer-service AI agent is configured to answer policy questions by retrieving from an enterprise knowledge base. An entry titled “Data Residency Requirements” was authored in January 2025. It states that customer data may be processed in any EU-compliant jurisdiction. In September 2025, the company signs a data processing agreement with a specific enterprise client requiring UK-only processing for that client’s accounts. The knowledge-base entry is not updated. No connection is established between the new contractual constraint and the entry that addresses the same topic.

A customer-service employee uses the AI agent to answer a data residency question from that client. The agent retrieves the January 2025 entry and responds that EU-compliant processing is available without restriction.

Governance consequence. The agent produced an answer accurate against its knowledge base. The knowledge base was accurate against the January 2025 policy. Neither was accurate against the September 2025 contractual reality. No model error occurred. No retrieval error occurred. The model acted on a decayed reference.

This case illustrates why AI agent governance cannot be reduced to model evaluation. The model retrieved the correct document from the knowledge base and produced an accurate response to that document’s contents. The failure was upstream: the reference the agent was authorized to act on had decayed.

Governing the agent — evaluating it, red-teaming it, tiering its risk — does not address the governance of the references it retrieves. Those references require a distinct and parallel governance layer.

Case 4 — Definition divergence across departments

Scenario. An enterprise AI governance committee uses the term “high-risk AI system” in two governance documents: the Risk Classification Framework, owned by the risk team, and the AI Procurement Policy, owned by legal. In 2024, both documents define “high-risk” using the same regulatory reference. In early 2026, the risk team updates its Classification Framework to incorporate a broader internal definition that includes any AI system interacting with employee data. The AI Procurement Policy is not updated. No shared changelog exists for the defined term. No notification is sent between teams.

A procurement decision is made in April 2026 using the AI Procurement Policy definition. A risk review of the same system, conducted the same week, uses the updated Classification Framework definition. The two reviews reach different conclusions about whether the system is high-risk.

Governance consequence. Two governed processes — each operating from a document that appears current — produce contradictory outputs about the same system at the same time. Neither document reveals the contradiction. The divergence is only visible at the governance layer, where the defined term itself is the reference that has decayed.

Definition divergence across departments is among the least visible forms of reference decay. Each department’s document appears complete and internally consistent. The failure is in the relationship between documents: a shared governance term has lost its shared definition, and no governed mechanism exists to detect, surface, or resolve the divergence.

The compound governance exposure

These four cases are not independent scenarios affecting different institutions. They are likely to appear together in any enterprise AI deployment of moderate complexity, because they arise from the same structural condition: AI documentation practices built for static, human-reviewed content applied to governance contexts that require version discipline, citation stability, and reference continuity.

The compound effect across a single enterprise AI deployment:

  • Model evaluation records cite model cards that have been revised without version history. The evaluation cannot be matched to the model state it evaluated.
  • Usage policies cited in governance documents have been updated in place. The governance documents do not reflect the current policy and cannot be distinguished from documents that do.
  • AI agents deployed in operational workflows retrieve knowledge-base content that has not been updated to reflect contractual, regulatory, or policy changes made after the knowledge base was last reviewed.
  • Governance terms used across risk, legal, compliance, and product functions have diverged in meaning without a mechanism to detect or reconcile the divergence.

The combined effect is a governance layer that appears complete — the documents exist, the URLs resolve, the processes run — but cannot be fully cited, reconstructed, or audited against the state that governed any specific prior decision.

What existing AI governance frameworks do not address

Existing enterprise AI governance frameworks — model cards, safety evaluations, responsible AI principles, risk tiering frameworks, deployment checklists — were designed to address model risk: whether the model behaves safely, fairly, and within defined constraints.

None of these frameworks require that the references those governance documents cite remain valid, versioned, or traceable after publication. A responsible AI policy may be exemplary in its model risk provisions and still cite a usage policy that has been revised in place, a regulatory reference that has since changed, and defined terms that have diverged across departments without controlled update.

This is not a deficiency in those frameworks — they address the problem they were designed for. It is a gap between the governance problem they address and the governance problem this brief describes:

Model governance asks whether the model is safe. Reference continuity asks whether the references the model acts on — and the governance documents that certify it — are still valid.

Reference continuity governance is the complementary discipline. It does not evaluate models. It governs the reference layer that models, governance processes, and audit functions depend on: the policies, definitions, knowledge bases, evaluation records, and documentation that must remain valid, versioned, citable, and internally consistent across time.

The four cases in this brief are not gaps in model governance. They are gaps in reference continuity governance — a discipline that existing AI governance frameworks do not address and were not designed to address.

What this brief does not claim

This brief presents operational cases of reference decay in AI documentation contexts. It does not:

  • Certify or assess any organization’s reference continuity posture.
  • Issue scores, ratings, maturity levels, or compliance designations.
  • Claim that existing AI governance frameworks are deficient — they address model risk, which this brief does not address.
  • Assert that reference decay is unique to AI documentation — it appears across all reference-grade institutional documentation.
  • Recommend specific products, vendors, or services for addressing reference continuity.
  • Claim that Evergreen Protocol eliminates reference decay — it governs a discipline, not an outcome guarantee.
