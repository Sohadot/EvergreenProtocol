Reference Continuity Readiness

This is a self-orientation guide for understanding the seven core readiness dimensions of Evergreen Protocol. It is not a certification, external assessment, registry, form submission path, or backend service.

How to use this guide

Use these dimensions to orient discussion about AI-era digital continuity before writing protocols, revising documentation, or building machine-readable reference surfaces. Each dimension describes what to look for, why it matters, and a question that helps expose reference continuity risk.

1. Version discipline

Definition
The practice of recording what changed in a reference system, when the change occurred, why it was made, and under whose authority.
Why it matters
Version discipline lets operators identify which reference governed a decision at a specific point in time.
Weak signal
Documents change in place with no visible version, owner, date, or rationale.
Strong signal
Material changes carry version, date, owner, summary, and a clear relation to earlier reference states.
Readiness question
Can a reader tell which version governed a decision made six months ago?

2. Citation stability

Definition
The ability to cite a reference, definition, protocol, or decision basis at a stable location and version.
Why it matters
Stable citation lets humans, AI agents, and institutional systems refer to the same source without depending on private context.
Weak signal
Links move, titles change, and old references disappear without trace.
Strong signal
Canonical URLs, versioned references, and citation guidance remain available over time.
Readiness question
Can an outside reader cite the governing reference without asking the original team for context?
Related reference
How to Cite

3. Evidence posture

Definition
The structural readiness to identify the governing reference, version, and responsible owner for protocols, decisions, and knowledge assets at the point of use.
Why it matters
Evidence posture determines whether an institution can reconstruct why a reference was trusted when it was used.
Weak signal
Evidence is scattered across messages, folders, tickets, and undocumented assumptions.
Strong signal
Evidence artifacts are named, reachable, owned, and tied to the relevant protocol version.
Readiness question
Can the organization show which artifacts supported a decision at the time it was made?
Related reference
Evidence posture

4. Definition control

Definition
The maintenance of bounded, versioned, citable definitions for terms that govern institutional behavior.
Why it matters
Definition control prevents local interpretations from silently changing the meaning of shared references.
Weak signal
The same term appears in multiple places with different meanings and no boundary note.
Strong signal
Governed terms have one canonical meaning, a version, a citation path, and clear scope boundaries.
Readiness question
Can readers locate the controlling definition for each term that changes behavior?
Related reference
Definitions

5. Changelog integrity

Definition
The reliability of a change record as a source of institutional memory: what changed, why, when, and under whose authority.
Why it matters
A trustworthy changelog lets a reference evolve without erasing prior states or leaving silent drift behind.
Weak signal
Change notes are vague, missing, or separated from the documents they govern.
Strong signal
The changelog records material changes with dates, rationale, version effect, and authority.
Readiness question
Can a reader understand the history of a reference without reconstructing it from memory?
Related reference
Changelog

6. Update governance

Definition
The rule structure for when references are reviewed, who may propose change, who decides, and what events trigger review.
Why it matters
Update governance keeps references aligned with reality as models, tools, teams, dependencies, and policy conditions change.
Weak signal
Updates happen only when someone notices a problem or when a document owner happens to revisit a page.
Strong signal
Review cadence, event triggers, decision rights, and exception handling are explicit.
Readiness question
What event would require this reference to be reviewed before ordinary cadence?
Related reference
Methodology

7. Machine-readable reference structure

Definition
A static, citable structure that lets search engines, AI agents, and institutional systems consume governed reference data.
Why it matters
AI-era continuity depends on references that are readable by both people and systems without turning the standard into a live service.
Weak signal
Machine consumers must scrape inconsistent pages or infer terms without stable JSON references.
Strong signal
Definitions, standard summary, and citation formats are available as static versioned JSON files.
Readiness question
Can a machine consumer locate the current reference data and the version-pinned reference data separately?
